## Practice communication on every match

Tō3 gives officials a live, full-duplex channel on the phones and headsets they already own. That makes crew communication available for ordinary community matches, not only the few fixtures that receive a radio kit.

Regular access gives officials more chances to practice the skills that matter: when to speak, what information the referee needs, how to keep it short, and when to stay quiet. An open mic also makes the crew more aware of unnecessary chatter, breathing, and background noise.

Push-to-talk radios still have a place, especially in noisy environments. Tō3 is not trying to copy that hardware. It fills the larger gap by helping officials build communication habits before they reach a level where dedicated radios are routinely available.

## What a crew can do

- **Talk in real time.** Hosts hear one another at the same time. Each official can mute from a compatible headset or from the phone.
- **Create and join events.** A host creates an event and shares a code. Other officials join as hosts, while coaches, assessors, or spectators can join as listen-only participants.
- **Check audio before the match.** Test the microphone and headset before taking the event live.
- **Take an intermission.** Pause the session without ending the event, then return when the crew is ready.
- **Record when the plan allows it.** An eligible primary host can choose to record. Everyone in the room sees a persistent notice, and hosts can download the completed MP3 after the event.
- **Use the same account on every device.** Sign in with Google or Apple and receive optional alerts when a saved event goes live.

## Why we built it

Justin and Marquise have worked across community rugby, Major League Rugby, international sevens, coaching, and referee development. They kept seeing the same problem: only a small number of crews had access to radio equipment, so most officials had no regular way to practice live communication.

Tō3 started with rugby, but the same need exists for tour guides, stage crews, coaches working across several fields, and race officials. The shared problem is simple: people need to stay in sync while they move, and dedicated hardware limits who gets to participate.
